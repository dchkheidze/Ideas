# Offers lens

Score: 4 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- The offer has parts a buyer can see: one standard brief, bids from verified crews, help choosing and setting up the contract, and money released in stages after sign-off. Staged release is the strongest built-in promise, because the owner sees a result before paying for it.
- The pain is concrete. An owner renovating a flat or rental in Tbilisi faces trust, price and delay risk on every job, and a standard brief removes the hardest step (writing a scope from scratch).
- The stack is cheap to build with the team's skills: a brief template, a bid comparison, a contract checklist and a payment schedule are product work, not trade work.
- There is a low-cost first test: 1 to 4 crews already willing to work informally.

## What would kill it (top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **No proof, so weak likelihood.** The promise rests on "verified crews" and safe staged payment, but nothing yet shows a crew finishing a job through this process. The crews are informal, there is no written partner agreement, and the meaning of "verified" is not written down. Likelihood is the value term the offer leans on most, and it is the weakest today.
2. **Market not yet proven reachable.** Size, owner spend, growth and acquisition channels are all unknown. The lens treats a market that cannot be reached cheaply as a pass. Until the cost of the first 20 to 100 owners is known, this is the largest open risk.
3. **Effort and two-sided cold start.** Owners must post a brief, review bids and manage stage payments. Crews bid only when there is volume, and owners post only when bids arrive. The owner may also see two charges (crew price plus service fee) against hiring a crew directly. If the total looks higher with no visible extra, the offer loses on price comparison, not on quality.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Who is the first buyer: an owner renovating their own flat, or a landlord turning over a rental? Which has the budget and the deadline?
- In the owner's words, what outcome do they want, and how many weeks until they would call it a success?
- What proof does an owner see before paying a crew? How many finished jobs do the 1 to 4 crews have, and can you show photos or references?
- What does a renovation cost the owner today, and what would they pay on top through the platform? Would they still choose you at that total?
- How will the first 20 owners find you, and what does each cost? Has any owner been asked to post a brief yet?
- If a crew stops or overruns mid-job, who pays, and can the budget cover that?
- How many verified crews must bid before an owner gets a real choice, and how many can you reach in Tbilisi today?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Demand test before most of the budget.** Real owners post a brief through a simple form or a manual process. Set the pass mark in writing beforehand: how many post, how many reply, how many would pay a deposit.
2. **Written pilot agreement** with the crews the pilot depends on, covering bid terms, stage sign-off and disputes. A short document is enough.
3. **Written definition of "verified"**, who checks it, and the proof an owner sees (past-job photos, references, any licence the lawyer says applies in Georgia).
4. **Acquisition cost per owner, measured in the test,** with a stated limit. If the cost of a signed owner exceeds the limit the founder sets, pause before scaling.
5. **Owner total cost tested against direct hiring.** Choose the fee structure after that test, not before.
6. **Money-flow choice made with the lawyer,** and the chosen option must run in the pilot within the $25k ceiling.
